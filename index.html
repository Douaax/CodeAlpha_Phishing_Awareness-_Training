What is Phishing?

Phishing is a cyberattack where criminals impersonate trusted entities to trick victims into revealing sensitive information like passwords, credit card numbers, or personal data.

🎣

Email Phishing

Mass emails pretending to be from banks, services, or employers. The most common form.

🎯

Spear Phishing

Targeted attacks using personal information to seem more credible to a specific individual.

🐋

Whaling

Spear phishing aimed specifically at executives, CEOs, or high-value targets.

📱

Smishing

Phishing via SMS text messages — "Your package couldn't be delivered, click here."

📞

Vishing

Voice phishing — phone calls from fake "bank representatives" or "tech support."

🔍

Search Engine Phishing

Fake websites ranked on Google ads to intercept victims searching for real services.

Recognizing Phishing Emails

Hover over the red ⚠ FLAG tags below to understand exactly why each part of this email is suspicious.

Inbox — Suspicious Email
FROM: security@paypa1-support.com ⚠ FLAG
TO: valued.customer@email.com ⚠ FLAG
SUBJECT: 🚨 URGENT: Your account has been SUSPENDED!! ⚠ FLAG

Dear Valued Customer, ⚠ FLAG

We have detected suspicious activity on your PayPal account. Your account has been temporarily limited. You must verify your information within 24 hours or your account will be permanently closed. ⚠ FLAG

Click below to restore access immediately:

→ Verify My Account Now (hover to see real URL) ⚠ FLAG

PayPal Security Team
© 2024 PayPal Inc. All rights reserved

1

Suspicious Sender Domain

Check the full email address — not just the display name. Look for subtle misspellings like paypa1.com vs paypal.com.

2

Urgency & Threats

"Act within 24 hours or lose access" — attackers use fear to bypass rational thinking and make you act fast.

3

Generic Greetings

"Dear Customer" instead of your name means it's a mass-sent attack not targeting you specifically.

4

Mismatched/Suspicious Links

Always hover over links before clicking. The displayed text may say "PayPal" but the URL points somewhere else entirely.

5

Grammar & Spelling Errors

Poor grammar and odd phrasing are common in phishing emails, often originating from non-native speakers.

6

Unexpected Attachments

Unsolicited attachments (.pdf, .docx, .zip) may contain malware. Never open attachments you weren't expecting.

Spotting Fake Websites

🔍 URL Inspector Tool

Try these examples:

paypa1-secure.ru paypal.com amazon-security-alert.com google.com netflix.com.verify-account.xyz
🔒

Check for HTTPS

HTTPS is necessary but NOT sufficient — phishing sites can also have SSL certificates. It doesn't mean the site is safe.

🌐

Examine the Domain Carefully

The real domain is the part just before the TLD. "paypal.com.login.xyz" — the real domain is xyz, not paypal.

📝

Look Up Domain Age

Phishing domains are often freshly registered. Use whois.domaintools.com to check how old a domain is.

⚠

Unusual TLDs

.ru, .xyz, .tk, .top for what claims to be a US bank or service is a major red flag.

Social Engineering Tactics

Attackers exploit human psychology, not technical vulnerabilities. Understanding these tactics is your best defense.

⏰

Urgency & Scarcity

Creates time pressure to prevent careful thinking. Forces snap decisions.

"Your account will be deleted in 2 hours unless you act NOW!"
😨

Fear & Intimidation

Threatens negative consequences — legal action, account closure, fines.

"The IRS has filed a lawsuit against you. Call immediately to avoid arrest."
🎁

Too Good to Be True

Lures victims with prizes, winnings, or unexpected rewards.

"Congratulations! You've been selected to receive a $1,000 Amazon gift card."
🤝

Authority & Trust

Impersonates executives, banks, government agencies, or IT departments.

"This is your CEO. Please wire $50,000 to this account urgently — it's confidential."
💬

Pretexting

Creates a fabricated scenario to justify the information request.

"Hi, I'm from IT. We're upgrading servers and need your login to migrate your data."
🔄

Reciprocity

Does something small for you first, then asks for something in return.

"I fixed your computer remotely for free! Just confirm your card for the warranty."

Real-World Cases

These are documented phishing attacks that caused massive damage — and the lessons learned from each.

2016 $100M+ Lost

Google & Facebook — Business Email Compromise

A Lithuanian fraudster impersonated Quanta Computer (a real vendor for both companies) and sent fake invoices over two years. Both tech giants paid over $100 million total before the scam was discovered.

✓ Lesson: Always verify large financial requests via a separate communication channel — call the vendor directly using a known number, never one from the email.
2016 DNC Email Leak

Democratic National Committee — Spear Phishing

A spear phishing email was sent to John Podesta (Hillary Clinton's campaign chairman) that looked like a Google security alert. An aide called it "legitimate" — it wasn't. The resulting credential theft led to massive email leaks that affected a US presidential election.

✓ Lesson: Enable two-factor authentication everywhere. A stolen password alone shouldn't be enough to compromise an account.
2020 Twitter Hack

Twitter — Vishing + Social Engineering

Attackers called Twitter employees, impersonating the IT department, and convinced them to hand over admin credentials. Accounts of Obama, Elon Musk, Apple, and others were hijacked to run a Bitcoin scam that netted over $120,000 in hours.

✓ Lesson: IT departments will NEVER ask for your credentials by phone. When in doubt, hang up and call your IT department using the official number.
2023 MGM Resorts — $100M Damage

MGM Resorts — 10-Minute Vishing Attack

Hackers found an MGM employee on LinkedIn, called the IT help desk impersonating them, and social-engineered their way into resetting credentials. A 10-minute phone call took down MGM's systems for over a week, causing $100M in losses.

✓ Lesson: Public info on LinkedIn and social media feeds attackers. Strict identity verification processes for IT requests are critical — never reset credentials based on a phone call alone.

Best Practices & Defense

Apply these habits to dramatically reduce your risk of falling victim to phishing attacks.

🔐

Use Multi-Factor Authentication

Enable MFA/2FA on every account. Even if attackers steal your password, they can't log in without the second factor.

🔑

Use a Password Manager

Password managers auto-fill only on the correct domain. If a site is fake, it won't auto-fill — your first warning sign.

🔍

Verify Before You Click

Hover over links. Go directly to websites by typing the URL yourself rather than clicking email links.

📞

Verify Out-of-Band

If you receive an urgent request (especially financial), verify by calling the sender using a known/official phone number.

🛡️

Keep Software Updated

Enable automatic updates for your OS, browser, and antivirus. Patches close vulnerabilities attackers exploit via malicious attachments.

🧠

When in Doubt, Don't

If something feels off — urgent tone, unexpected request, unknown sender — pause. No legitimate organization requires you to act in minutes.

📊

Report Suspicious Emails

Use your email client's "Report Phishing" button. This helps protect everyone in your organization and trains spam filters.

🌐

Check Domain Registration

Use tools like whois.domaintools.com to check if a domain was registered recently — phishing domains rarely last more than a few weeks.
